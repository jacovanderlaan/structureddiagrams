Lineage & governance

Impact Analysis

Lineage traversed backwards: what breaks if I change this? The question every engineer asks before touching anything, and the one most platforms cannot answer.

Lineage answers "where did this come from". Impact analysis answers "what breaks if I change it". Same graph, opposite direction — and the reverse question is the one people actually ask before doing anything.

Sometimes called *reverse lineage*, *downstream analysis* or *dependency analysis*. The naming varies; the move does not. Start at a node, walk the edges forward instead of back, and collect everything that would be affected.

Why it matters more than lineage

Lineage is retrospective. It explains a number after someone has questioned it — useful, occasionally urgent, but a report on the past.

Impact analysis is prospective, and it gates action. Nobody drops a column, renames a field, changes a filter or deprecates a source without first asking what depends on it. When the platform cannot answer, the question does not disappear — it gets answered badly:

- by asking around, and getting the recollection of whoever happens to be present - by grepping the codebase and hoping the SQL is not generated - by shipping it and finding out on Monday - most often, by not changing it at all

That last one is the real cost. A platform without impact analysis does not become safer; it becomes rigid. Nobody dares touch anything, so debt accumulates and the model calcifies around whatever was true three years ago. Fear of unknown consequences is the most expensive form of technical debt, because it is invisible in every metric.

The questions it answers

| Question | What it traverses | |---|---| | What consumes this source table? | entities downstream of a source | | Where is this transformation function used? | mappings referencing a function | | What does this filter exclude, and for whom? | entities affected by a predicate | | Which reports touch personal data? | classification propagated forward | | If this job fails tonight, what is stale tomorrow? | the execution DAG, downstream | | Can I deprecate this? | the absence of consumers — the answer nobody can give confidently |

That last row is the quiet one. Deprecation is impossible without impact analysis, because you cannot prove a negative by asking around. Which is why data platforms accumulate tables nobody has read since 2019 and nobody dares delete.

Blast radius, not the full graph

The naive implementation returns everything reachable downstream, which for any real platform is most of the platform. Technically correct, practically another wall.

What makes it usable is bounded traversal:

- Depth — one hop shows direct consumers; three shows the realistic blast radius; unbounded shows the warehouse. - Direction — downstream (what breaks) or upstream (what would I need to fix first). Both matter; showing both at once shows neither. - Severity — a column referenced in a `WHERE` clause breaks differently from one merely selected. Not every edge is the same edge. - Aggregation — "14 reports in Finance" is more actionable than fourteen unfamiliar report names.

A good impact analysis is a short, ranked list with a picture attached, not a graph dump.

Why generated metadata is the precondition

Impact analysis is only as good as the edges it can see. That is the whole difficulty, and it is why so many catalogues promise it and disappoint.

If transformation logic lives in hand-written SQL, the platform has to parse it correctly — every dialect, every dynamic query, every stored procedure — to know that a column is used. Coverage is partial by construction, and partial impact analysis is arguably worse than none: it produces a confident, incomplete answer, and confidence is what people act on.

When the mapping is modelled rather than buried — the SELECT list as data, the join as a row, the filter as a predicate the platform knows about — the dependency graph is not inferred. It is read. Column-level impact stops being a parsing achievement and becomes a query.

⚠️ The honest caveat: nothing sees consumers outside the platform. A spreadsheet someone built from a CSV export, a dashboard on a personal database, a script on someone's laptop — these are real dependencies and no impact analysis will find them. Treat the answer as *what is knowable*, not *everything that exists*, and keep the [seams declared](concept-decoupling) so the unknowable set stays small.

Where it belongs

Not in a portal someone visits occasionally. Impact analysis earns its keep when it is in the path of the change: in the pull request, next to the model, on the diagram. Click an entity in a generated ERD and see its consumers (Clickable Diagrams). Open a PR that drops a column and have the affected reports listed in the check.

An answer available at the moment of the decision changes the decision. An answer available in a tool you have to remember to open does not.
