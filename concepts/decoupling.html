Architecture

Decoupling

Systems meet only at agreed interfaces — inbound and outbound — so each side can change independently. The contract is the seam, and it is monitored.

Systems meet only at agreed interfaces — never in each other's internals. Every system has an inbound side (what it accepts) and an outbound side (what it promises), and between two systems sits a data contract: agreed up front, versioned, and continuously monitored.

That last word is what separates decoupling from a diagram. Boundaries drawn on a slide are not boundaries; boundaries that break a build when violated are.

Why it is a BTM key concept

Coupling is the reason modelling work does not survive contact with reality. A model that reaches into another system's tables inherits that system's every change, and the dependency is invisible until something breaks at 3am. Decoupling makes the dependency explicit, named, and testable — the same instinct as Just to Be Clear, applied to system boundaries instead of definitions.

It is also what makes independent movement possible. Structure and movement only unify if the seams hold: you cannot model transformation across systems that reach into each other arbitrarily. Decoupling is the precondition for the rest of BTM, not a nice-to-have alongside it.

The anatomy

| Part | What it is | What breaks without it | |---|---|---| | Inbound interface | What this system accepts, in what shape, at what quality | Upstream mess leaks in and shapes your model — see Anti-Corruption Layer | | Outbound interface | What this system promises to deliver | Consumers reverse-engineer your internals and freeze them by accident | | The contract | The agreement between the two: shape, semantics, quality, cadence, ownership | "It used to work" — with nobody accountable for the change | | Monitoring | Continuous checks that the contract still holds | The contract becomes documentation, and documentation rots |

What decoupling is not

Not isolation. Decoupled systems talk constantly — they just talk through the front door. The goal is not fewer connections but *declared* ones.

Not microservices. This is an architectural property, not a deployment style. A monolith with clean internal contracts is more decoupled than a hundred services reaching into each other's databases.

Not free. Every seam is a thing to design, version and monitor. Draw boundaries where change actually happens — around ownership and rate of change — not everywhere you can.

The failure it prevents

The tightly coupled system is the one nobody dares to change. Every modification requires knowing what else will break, so knowledge of the whole becomes a precondition for touching any part of it — which is a Bus Factor of One dressed up as architecture. Decoupling is how a system stays modifiable by people who did not build it.
